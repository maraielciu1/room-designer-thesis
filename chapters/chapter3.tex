
\chapter{3D Gaussian Splatting}
\label{ch:theory2}

Before introducing 3D Gaussian Splatting it helps to recall the volumetric
representation it replaces. Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) showed that a scene can
be encoded as a continuous volumetric function approximated by a neural network,
enabling high-quality view synthesis from posed
images~\cite{mildenhall2020nerf}. Training required hours of GPU time and
rendering was far from real-time, motivating the shift towards explicit,
rasterisable representations. \textbf{3D Gaussian Splatting} (3DGS), introduced by Kerbl et al.\
\cite{kerbl20233dgs}, replaces the neural field by a set of explicit
anisotropic Gaussians in space. Kerbl et al.\ show that this representation delivers
high visual quality together with real-time rendering: their method matches
or exceeds prior radiance-field quality while running at interactive frame rates on
desktop GPUs~\cite{kerbl20233dgs}. In this thesis, 3DGS is used as an imported
representation ('.ply' files) rendered with a custom Metal pipeline for
comparison and viewing; mesh-based USDZ assets remain the medium for
interactive furniture editing.

This chapter first explains what each Gaussian stores and how it is projected and
composited into an image. It then outlines the differentiable training loop used in
classical 3DGS, analyses why that loop is impractical for mobile capture, and contrasts
mesh-based and Gaussian-based assets in the application. Finally, it introduces SHARP
as a feedforward single-image alternative and describes how both splat paths are
integrated on iOS.

\section{Representation: 3D Gaussian Primitives}
\label{sec:3dgs_representation}

\paragraph{Intuition before formalization.}
Before introducing equations, it helps to view each Gaussian as a soft, oriented
``blob'' of radiance in space rather than as a hard surface sample. Many overlapping
blobs approximate geometry and appearance without explicit triangle connectivity. The
parameters below specify where each blob is centred, how elongated and rotated it is,
and how strongly it contributes colour and opacity when projected onto the image.

Each primitive stores a mean $\boldsymbol{\mu} \in \mathbb{R}^3$, a covariance
$\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ describing elongation and orientation, an opacity $\alpha$, and
colour parameters. Following Kerbl et al.~\cite{kerbl20233dgs}, $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ is
built from a rotation matrix $\mathbf{R}$ (from unit quaternion $\mathbf{q}$) and
diagonal scale matrix $\mathbf{S}=\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{s})$ with positive
entries:
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\Sigma} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{S}^\top\mathbf{R}^\top .
\label{eq:3dgs_covariance}
\end{equation}
Colour may be view-dependent via spherical harmonics; the implementation described
later also accepts per-Gaussian RGB in linear space. The spatial falloff of a single
primitive is
\begin{equation}
G(\mathbf{x}) = \exp\!\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^\top
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1} (\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})\right).
\label{eq:3dgs_gaussian}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{x}$ is a point in world space. Large values of $G(\mathbf{x})$ indicate
that $\mathbf{x}$ lies near the centre of the primitive along its principal axes.

Having defined the 3D primitives, the next section explains how they become pixels in a
rendered image.


\section{Projection, Rasterisation, and Real-Time Rendering}
\label{sec:3dgs_splatting}

Rendering splats each Gaussian onto the image~\cite{kerbl20233dgs}. Conceptually, the
pipeline proceeds in four steps:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Project} the mean $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ with the camera extrinsic and
    intrinsic matrices.

    \item \textbf{Project covariance} to a $2\times2$ image-space covariance
    $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}'$ using the Jacobian $\mathbf{J}$ of the perspective mapping
    evaluated at $\boldsymbol{\mu}$, yielding an elliptical footprint on the image plane.

    \item \textbf{Sort} primitives (globally or per tile) for approximate depth order.

    \item \textbf{Rasterise} with alpha blending, accumulating colour and transmittance
    along rays.
\end{enumerate}

Kerbl et al.\ describe tile-based culling and sorting strategies that make the above
pipeline practical for \textbf{real-time} use~\cite{kerbl20233dgs}. At each pixel,
ordered compositing follows the familiar over-operator,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{c}_{\mathrm{out}} = \alpha \cdot \mathbf{c}_{\mathrm{G}}
+ (1-\alpha)\,\mathbf{c}_{\mathrm{in}},
\label{eq:3dgs_alpha_blend}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{c}_{\mathrm{G}}$ is the Gaussian colour (possibly view-dependent),
$\alpha$ is opacity after evaluating the projected footprint, and
$\mathbf{c}_{\mathrm{in}}$ is the colour accumulated from Gaussians farther from the
camera. In this application, imported '.ply' splats are rendered by a custom
Metal orbit viewer; expensive training or feedforward inference remains outside the
iOS runtime.

Because splat parameters must be fitted to photographs in classical 3DGS, the
following section summarises the differentiable optimisation loop.


\section{Differentiable Rendering and Optimisation}
\label{sec:3dgs_differentiable}

To fit Gaussians to photographs, the renderer must be \textbf{differentiable}. A
photometric loss $\mathcal{L}$ (typically $\mathcal{L}_2$ or a combination with
structural terms) compares rendered and ground-truth images. Gradients
$\partial \mathcal{L}/\partial \mathbf{c}_{\mathrm{out}}$ propagate backwards through:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Alpha compositing} (Equation~\ref{eq:3dgs_alpha_blend}): partial
    derivatives adjust per-Gaussian colour and opacity.

    \item \textbf{Rasterisation}: pixels influenced by a Gaussian receive scattered
    gradients.

    \item \textbf{Projection}: errors on the image plane differentiate through the
    projective transform and through the Jacobian $\mathbf{J}$ that maps 3D covariance
    $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ to 2D covariance $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}'$, updating
    $\boldsymbol{\mu}$, scales $\mathbf{s}$, and rotations $\mathbf{q}$.
\end{enumerate}

This is structurally analogous to other differentiable rendering pipelines:
screen-space residuals are tied to 3D parameters through a projection map. Optimisation
typically alternates gradient steps with adaptive density control (splitting
large Gaussians, cloning under-reconstructed regions, culling near-transparent ones) ~\cite{kerbl20233dgs}.
Initialisation often starts from a sparse point cloud (e.g.\ from COLMAP
structure-from-motion), linking 3DGS training to the same family of geometry
estimation as photogrammetry-based capture.

\paragraph{Training loop in steps.}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Initialise Gaussians from sparse SfM points and estimated camera poses.
    \item Render the current scene from a sampled training viewpoint.
    \item Compute $\mathcal{L}$ between rendered and reference images.
    \item Backpropagate through compositing, rasterisation, and projection to update
    $\boldsymbol{\mu}$, $\mathbf{s}$, $\mathbf{q}$, $\alpha$, and appearance parameters.
    \item Apply densification/pruning heuristics and repeat until convergence.
\end{enumerate}

This loop yields high visual quality but requires many views, reliable calibration, and
substantial GPU time. In the present thesis workflow, full optimisation is performed
off-device; the mobile app consumes only resulting '.ply' assets. The next
section explains why classical training is a poor match for user-initiated mobile
capture.


\section{Limitations of Classical 3DGS for Mobile and Real-Time Capture}
\label{sec:3dgs_limitations}

Despite its visual quality and real-time \emph{rendering} speed, the original 3DGS
pipeline ~\cite{kerbl20233dgs} carries practical constraints that limit applicability in
a mobile, user-facing scenario:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Multi-view requirement.} Classical 3DGS requires photographs from
    multiple, carefully distributed viewpoints, typically 50--200+ images. This makes
    it unsuitable for capturing a scene from a single photo or from limited handheld
    movement.

    \item \textbf{Dependency on SfM.} Before training, camera poses must be estimated by
    running structure-from-motion software such as COLMAP~\cite{SSF16}. This offline step
    can take minutes to hours and requires sufficient feature overlap between views.

    \item \textbf{Per-scene optimisation.} Each new scene requires hundreds of thousands
    of gradient-descent iterations on a GPU. A model trained on one room cannot
    reconstruct another without retraining.

    \item \textbf{Adaptive densification overhead.} Splitting, cloning, and pruning
    Gaussians during training improves quality but complicates porting the full pipeline
    to mobile hardware.

    \item \textbf{Relative scale.} Initialisation from a COLMAP sparse point cloud
    yields geometry up to similarity only. Without metric depth or a known baseline,
    absolute scale is unknown.
\end{itemize}

These constraints collectively make the classical \emph{training} pipeline impractical
for real-time, user-initiated mobile capture. They do not invalidate splats as a
viewing medium: pre-trained or server-generated '.ply' files remain
useful benchmarks. They motivate feedforward alternatives such as SHARP (Section~\ref{sec:sharp})
and clarify why mesh-based USDZ assets remain the authoring substrate (next section).


\section{Meshes versus Gaussians: Editability and Role in the Application}
\label{sec:3dgs_vs_mesh}

\textbf{Meshes (USDZ from Object Capture)} are products of an on-device pipeline
analogous to multi-view stereo plus surface reconstruction (e.g.\ Poisson
reconstruction ~\cite{kazhdan2006poisson} in the classical photogrammetry literature).
Such meshes expose {explicit connectivity: faces can be clipped, simplified, or
rigged; axis-aligned bounding boxes align naturally with RoomPlan's boxy furniture
detections; RealityKit loads USDZ natively. \textbf{3D Gaussians}~\cite{kerbl20233dgs}
do not define a thin surface: ``moving'' an object means transforming thousands of
primitives coherently; boolean cuts and physical simulation are ill-defined without
conversion. Blender export in this project stages mesh-based room structure and USDZ
furniture; splats remain in-app benchmarks unless imported manually in Blender.

The product decision in this thesis is therefore:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Authoring and replacement}: USDZ meshes only.

    \item \textbf{Visualisation and comparison}: optional '.ply' splats via
    Metal, following the real-time splatting paradigm of Kerbl et al.~\cite{kerbl20233dgs}.

    \item \textbf{Training}: off-device in the current system; on-device or fast
    training remains future work~\cite{Guo26}.
\end{itemize}

\section{SHARP: Feedforward Single-Image 3D Gaussian Splatting}
\label{sec:sharp}

\textbf{SHARP} (\emph{Sharp Monocular View Synthesis})~\cite{sharp-monocular-view} is a
method developed at Apple and presented at ICLR~2025. Given a \emph{single} RGB
photograph, SHARP predicts the full set of 3D Gaussian parameters for the depicted
scene in one forward pass through a neural network, with no iterative optimisation, no
multi-view input, and no SfM preprocessing. The output is a standard '.ply' that
renders in real time via the same splatting pipeline as classical 3DGS
(Equations~\ref{eq:3dgs_gaussian}--\ref{eq:3dgs_alpha_blend}). Inference takes under one
second on a modern discrete GPU.

The method builds on a pretrained monocular depth estimator (Depth
Pro  ~\cite{BDGZ25}) to initialise $\approx 1.2\times10^6$ Gaussians from the predicted
metric depth map, then refines all Gaussian attributes through a learned decoder. This
design yields three properties relevant to this thesis: (1)~real-time novel-view
rendering via standard 3DGS rasterisation; (2)~absolute metric scale, enabling
physically meaningful camera displacements compatible with AR sensors; and
(3)~zero-shot generalisation across indoor and outdoor scenes

SHARP does not replace mesh authoring; it complements it by reducing capture friction
for radiance-style previews. The following section describes how both splat paths are
wired into the iOS application.


SHARP is integrated as a supplementary capture mode alongside RoomPlan and 
Object Capture, following a lightweight client--server architecture that keeps 
the heavy neural inference off the device entirely.

On the server side, a Flask application running on a Mac on the local network 
loads the pretrained SHARP weights once at startup and waits for incoming 
requests. When the iOS client posts a JPEG image, the server runs the SHARP 
inference pipeline and applies an opacity-based pruning step before responding. 
This pruning is necessary because the raw SHARP output contains approximately 
$1.2 \times 10^6$ Gaussians, producing a file of around 66\,MB, far too 
large for interactive depth-sorted rendering on a mobile device. By retaining 
only the $3 \times 10^5$ Gaussians with the highest opacity scores, the server 
reduces the payload to approximately 18\,MB while preserving the visually 
significant structure of the scene.

On the client side, a dedicated capture view allows the user to photograph 
any scene using the standard iOS image picker. The selected frame is 
compressed to JPEG and sent to the server over the local network. One 
practical challenge during development was timeout handling: the default 
iOS networking session imposes a 60-second limit on requests, which is 
insufficient when the server must compile Metal shaders on first run. A 
custom session configured with a 360-second timeout resolves this. Once 
the server responds, the returned '.ply' file is saved to disk and 
opened immediately in the same Metal-based orbit viewer used for 
offline-trained Gaussian splats, so the experience is seamless to the user 
regardless of which capture mode produced the scene.

This positions the application to demonstrate the full reconstruction spectrum:
\textbf{RoomPlan} (parametric, real-time) $\rightarrow$
\textbf{Object Capture} (photogrammetric mesh, on-device) $\rightarrow$
\textbf{3DGS} (optimisation-based, external GPU) $\rightarrow$
\textbf{SHARP} (feedforward neural, single image, near real-time) $\rightarrow$
\textbf{Blender export} (staged mesh hand-off).


3D Gaussian Splatting offers an efficient explicit radiance representation: each
primitive is parameterised by $\boldsymbol{\mu}$, $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$, $\alpha$, and
appearance; projection via $\mathbf{J}$ and alpha compositing
(Equation~\ref{eq:3dgs_alpha_blend}) enable real-time viewing; and differentiable
optimisation fits splats to multi-view photographs. Classical training nonetheless
remains poorly matched to mobile, user-initiated capture because of data, pose, compute,
and scale constraints. This thesis therefore keeps USDZ meshes for interactive room
design and uses imported '.ply' splats, including SHARP outputs pruned for
mobile performance, for visual comparison in a unified Metal viewer.
